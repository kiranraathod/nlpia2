**Computer science** is the study of [computation](/wiki/Computation "Computation"), [information](/wiki/Information "Information"), and [automation](/wiki/Automation "Automation").[[1]](#cite_note-YorkDefinition-1)[[2]](#cite_note-MITAutomated-2)[[3]](#cite_note-DenningAutomated-3) Included broadly in [the sciences](/wiki/Science "Science"), computer science spans [theoretical disciplines](/wiki/Theoretical_computer_science "Theoretical computer science") (such as [algorithms](/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm"), [theory of computation](/wiki/Theory_of_computation "Theory of computation"), and [information theory](/wiki/Information_theory "Information theory")) to [applied disciplines](/wiki/Applied_science "Applied science") (including the design and implementation of [hardware](/wiki/Computer_architecture "Computer architecture") and [software](/wiki/Software_engineering "Software engineering")).[[4]](#cite_note-4)[[5]](#cite_note-5)[[6]](#cite_note-6) An expert in the field is known as a [computer scientist](/wiki/Computer_scientist "Computer scientist").

Algorithms and [data structures](/wiki/Data_structure "Data structure") are central to computer science.[[7]](#cite_note-7) The theory of computation concerns abstract [models of computation](/wiki/Models_of_computation "Models of computation") and general classes of [problems](/wiki/Computational_problem "Computational problem") that can be solved using them. The fields of [cryptography](/wiki/Cryptography "Cryptography") and [computer security](/wiki/Computer_security "Computer security") involve studying the means for secure communication and preventing [security vulnerabilities](/wiki/Security_vulnerabilities "Security vulnerabilities"). [Computer graphics](/wiki/Computer_graphics_(computer_science) "Computer graphics (computer science)") and [computational geometry](/wiki/Computational_geometry "Computational geometry") address the generation of images. [Programming language theory](/wiki/Programming_language_theory "Programming language theory") considers different ways to describe computational processes, and [database](/wiki/Database "Database") theory concerns the management of repositories of data. [Human–computer interaction](/wiki/Human%E2%80%93computer_interaction "Human–computer interaction") investigates the interfaces through which humans and computers interact, and software engineering focuses on the design and principles behind developing software. Areas such as [operating systems](/wiki/Operating_system "Operating system"), [networks](/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network") and [embedded systems](/wiki/Embedded_system "Embedded system") investigate the principles and design behind [complex systems](/wiki/Complex_system "Complex system"). Computer architecture describes the construction of computer components and computer-operated equipment. [Artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") and [machine learning](/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning") aim to synthesize goal-orientated processes such as problem-solving, decision-making, environmental adaptation, [planning](/wiki/Automated_planning_and_scheduling "Automated planning and scheduling") and learning found in humans and animals. Within artificial intelligence, [computer vision](/wiki/Computer_vision "Computer vision") aims to understand and process image and video data, while [natural language processing](/wiki/Natural_language_processing "Natural language processing") aims to understand and process textual and linguistic data.

The fundamental concern of computer science is determining what can and cannot be automated.[[2]](#cite_note-MITAutomated-2)[[8]](#cite_note-8)[[3]](#cite_note-DenningAutomated-3)[[9]](#cite_note-9)[[10]](#cite_note-10) The [Turing Award](/wiki/Turing_Award "Turing Award") is generally recognized as the highest distinction in computer science.[[11]](#cite_note-11)[[12]](#cite_note-12)

[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz") (1646–1716) developed [logic](/wiki/Logic "Logic") in a [binary number](/wiki/Binary_number "Binary number") system and has been called the "founder of computer science".[[13]](#cite_note-13)

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Babbage40.png/250px-Babbage40.png)](/wiki/File:Babbage40.png)

[Charles Babbage](/wiki/Charles_Babbage "Charles Babbage") is sometimes referred to as the "father of computing".[[14]](#cite_note-14)

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Ada_lovelace.jpg/250px-Ada_lovelace.jpg)](/wiki/File:Ada_lovelace.jpg)

[Ada Lovelace](/wiki/Ada_Lovelace "Ada Lovelace") published the first [algorithm](/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm") intended for processing on a computer.[[15]](#cite_note-15)

The earliest foundations of what would become computer science predate the invention of the modern [digital computer](/wiki/Digital_computer "Digital computer"). Machines for calculating fixed numerical tasks such as the [abacus](/wiki/Abacus "Abacus") have existed since antiquity, aiding in computations such as multiplication and division. [Algorithms](/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm") for performing computations have existed since antiquity, even before the development of sophisticated computing equipment.[[16]](#cite_note-16)

[Wilhelm Schickard](/wiki/Wilhelm_Schickard "Wilhelm Schickard") designed and constructed the first working [mechanical calculator](/wiki/Mechanical_calculator "Mechanical calculator") in 1623.[[17]](#cite_note-17) In 1673, [Gottfried Leibniz](/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz") demonstrated a digital mechanical calculator, called the [Stepped Reckoner](/wiki/Stepped_Reckoner "Stepped Reckoner").[[18]](#cite_note-18)[[19]](#cite_note-19) Leibniz may be considered the first [computer scientist](/wiki/Computer_scientist "Computer scientist") and information theorist, because of various reasons, including the fact that he documented the binary number system. In 1820, [Thomas de Colmar](/wiki/Thomas_de_Colmar "Thomas de Colmar") launched the mechanical calculator industry[[note 1]](#cite_note-20) when he invented his simplified [arithmometer](/wiki/Arithmometer "Arithmometer"), the first calculating machine strong enough and reliable enough to be used daily in an office environment. [Charles Babbage](/wiki/Charles_Babbage "Charles Babbage") started the design of the first *automatic mechanical calculator*, his [Difference Engine](/wiki/Difference_Engine "Difference Engine"), in 1822, which eventually gave him the idea of the first *programmable mechanical calculator*, his [Analytical Engine](/wiki/Analytical_Engine "Analytical Engine").[[20]](#cite_note-21) He started developing this machine in 1834, and "in less than two years, he had sketched out many of the [salient](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Special:Search/salient "wikt:Special:Search/salient") features of the modern computer".[[21]](#cite_note-Hyman1982-22) "A crucial step was the adoption of a punched card system derived from the [Jacquard loom](/wiki/Jacquard_loom "Jacquard loom")"[[21]](#cite_note-Hyman1982-22) making it infinitely programmable.[[note 2]](#cite_note-23) In 1843, during the translation of a French article on the Analytical Engine, [Ada Lovelace](/wiki/Ada_Lovelace "Ada Lovelace") wrote, in one of the many notes she included, an algorithm to compute the [Bernoulli numbers](/wiki/Bernoulli_number "Bernoulli number"), which is considered to be the first published algorithm ever specifically tailored for implementation on a computer.[[22]](#cite_note-24) Around 1885, [Herman Hollerith](/wiki/Herman_Hollerith "Herman Hollerith") invented the [tabulator](/wiki/Tabulating_machine "Tabulating machine"), which used [punched cards](/wiki/Punched_card "Punched card") to process statistical information; eventually his company became part of [IBM](/wiki/IBM "IBM"). Following Babbage, although unaware of his earlier work, [Percy Ludgate](/wiki/Percy_Ludgate "Percy Ludgate") in 1909 published[[23]](#cite_note-25) the 2nd of the only two designs for mechanical analytical engines in history. In 1914, the Spanish engineer [Leonardo Torres Quevedo](/wiki/Leonardo_Torres_Quevedo "Leonardo Torres Quevedo") published his *Essays on Automatics*,[[24]](#cite_note-LTQ1914es-26) and designed, inspired by Babbage, a theoretical electromechanical calculating machine which was to be controlled by a read-only program. The paper also introduced the idea of [floating-point arithmetic](/wiki/Floating-point_arithmetic "Floating-point arithmetic").[[25]](#cite_note-27)[[26]](#cite_note-28) In 1920, to celebrate the 100th anniversary of the invention of the arithmometer, Torres presented in Paris the [Electromechanical Arithmometer](/wiki/Leonardo_Torres_y_Quevedo#Analytical_machines "Leonardo Torres y Quevedo"), a prototype that demonstrated the feasibility of an electromechanical analytical engine,[[27]](#cite_note-29) on which commands could be typed and the results printed automatically.[[28]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERandell19826,_11–13-30) In 1937, one hundred years after Babbage's impossible dream, [Howard Aiken](/wiki/Howard_Aiken "Howard Aiken") convinced IBM, which was making all kinds of punched card equipment and was also in the calculator business[[29]](#cite_note-31) to develop his giant programmable calculator, the [ASCC/Harvard Mark I](/wiki/Harvard_Mark_I "Harvard Mark I"), based on Babbage's Analytical Engine, which itself used [punched cards](/wiki/Punched_cards "Punched cards") and a [central processing unit](/wiki/Central_processing_unit "Central processing unit"). When the machine was finished, some hailed it as "Babbage's dream come true".[[30]](#cite_note-32)

During the 1940s, with the development of new and more powerful [computing](/wiki/Computing "Computing") machines such as the [Atanasoff–Berry computer](/wiki/Atanasoff%E2%80%93Berry_computer "Atanasoff–Berry computer") and [ENIAC](/wiki/ENIAC "ENIAC"), the term *computer* came to refer to the machines rather than their human predecessors.[[31]](#cite_note-33) As it became clear that computers could be used for more than just mathematical calculations, the field of computer science broadened to study [computation](/wiki/Computation "Computation") in general. In 1945, IBM founded the [Watson Scientific Computing Laboratory](/wiki/Watson_Scientific_Computing_Laboratory "Watson Scientific Computing Laboratory") at [Columbia University](/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). The renovated fraternity house on Manhattan's West Side was IBM's first laboratory devoted to pure science. The lab is the forerunner of IBM's Research Division, which today operates research facilities around the world.[[32]](#cite_note-34) Ultimately, the close relationship between IBM and Columbia University was instrumental in the emergence of a new scientific discipline, with Columbia offering one of the first academic-credit courses in computer science in 1946.[[33]](#cite_note-35) Computer science began to be established as a distinct academic discipline in the 1950s and early 1960s.[[34]](#cite_note-Denning-36)[[35]](#cite_note-37) The world's first computer science degree program, the [Cambridge Diploma in Computer Science](/wiki/Cambridge_Diploma_in_Computer_Science "Cambridge Diploma in Computer Science"), began at the [University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory](/wiki/University_of_Cambridge_Computer_Laboratory "University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory") in 1953. The first computer science department in the United States was formed at [Purdue University](/wiki/Purdue_University "Purdue University") in 1962.[[36]](#cite_note-38) Since practical computers became available, many applications of computing have become distinct areas of study in their own rights.

See also: [History of computing](/wiki/History_of_computing "History of computing") and [History of informatics](/wiki/History_of_informatics "History of informatics")

Etymology and scope
-------------------

See also: [Informatics § Etymology](/wiki/Informatics#Etymology "Informatics")

Although first proposed in 1956,[[37]](#cite_note-Tedre2014-39) the term "computer science" appears in a 1959 article in *[Communications of the ACM](/wiki/Communications_of_the_ACM "Communications of the ACM")*,[[38]](#cite_note-Fine_1959-40) in which Louis Fein argues for the creation of a *Graduate School in Computer Sciences* analogous to the creation of [Harvard Business School](/wiki/Harvard_Business_School "Harvard Business School") in 1921.[[39]](#cite_note-41) Louis justifies the name by arguing that, like [management science](/wiki/Management_science "Management science"), the subject is applied and interdisciplinary in nature, while having the characteristics typical of an academic discipline.[[38]](#cite_note-Fine_1959-40) This effort, and those of others such as [numerical analyst](/wiki/Numerical_analyst "Numerical analyst") [George Forsythe](/wiki/George_Forsythe "George Forsythe"), were successful, and universities went on to create such departments, starting with Purdue in 1962.[[40]](#cite_note-42) Despite its name, a significant amount of computer science does not involve the study of computers themselves. Because of this, several alternative names have been proposed.[[41]](#cite_note-43) Certain departments of major universities prefer the term *computing science*, to emphasize precisely that difference. Danish scientist [Peter Naur](/wiki/Peter_Naur "Peter Naur") suggested the term *datalogy*,[[42]](#cite_note-44) to reflect the fact that the scientific discipline revolves around data and data treatment, while not necessarily involving computers. The first scientific institution to use the term was the Department of Datalogy at the University of Copenhagen, founded in 1969, with Peter Naur being the first professor in datalogy. The term is used mainly in the Scandinavian countries. An alternative term, also proposed by Naur, is [data science](/wiki/Data_science "Data science"); this is now used for a [multi-disciplinary](/wiki/Multi-disciplinary "Multi-disciplinary") field of data analysis, including statistics and databases.

In the early days of computing, a number of terms for the practitioners of the field of computing were suggested (albeit facetiously) in the *Communications of the ACM*—*turingineer*, *turologist*, *flow-charts-man*, *applied meta-mathematician*, and *applied [epistemologist](/wiki/Epistemologist "Epistemologist")*.[[43]](#cite_note-45) Three months later in the same journal, *comptologist* was suggested, followed next year by *hypologist*.[[44]](#cite_note-46) The term *computics* has also been suggested.[[45]](#cite_note-47) In Europe, terms derived from contracted translations of the expression "automatic information" (e.g. "*informazione automatica*" in Italian) or "information and mathematics" are often used, e.g. *informatique* (French), *Informatik* (German), *informatica* (Italian, Dutch), *informática* (Spanish, Portuguese), *informatika* ([Slavic languages](/wiki/Slavic_languages "Slavic languages") and [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language")) or *pliroforiki* (πληροφορική, which means informatics) in [Greek](/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language"). Similar words have also been adopted in the UK (as in the [School of Informatics, University of Edinburgh](/wiki/School_of_Informatics,_University_of_Edinburgh "School of Informatics, University of Edinburgh")).[[46]](#cite_note-48) "In the U.S., however, [informatics](/wiki/Informatics "Informatics") is linked with applied computing, or computing in the context of another domain."[[47]](#cite_note-49)

A folkloric quotation, often attributed to—but almost certainly not first formulated by—[Edsger Dijkstra](/wiki/Edsger_Dijkstra "Edsger Dijkstra"), states that "computer science is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes."[[note 3]](#cite_note-50) The design and deployment of computers and computer systems is generally considered the province of disciplines other than computer science. For example, the study of computer hardware is usually considered part of [computer engineering](/wiki/Computer_engineering "Computer engineering"), while the study of commercial [computer systems](/wiki/Computer_system "Computer system") and their deployment is often called information technology or [information systems](/wiki/Information_system "Information system"). However, there has been exchange of ideas between the various computer-related disciplines. Computer science research also often intersects other disciplines, such as [cognitive science](/wiki/Cognitive_science "Cognitive science"), [linguistics](/wiki/Computational_linguistics "Computational linguistics"), [mathematics](/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [physics](/wiki/Physics "Physics"), [biology](/wiki/Biology "Biology"), [Earth science](/wiki/Earth_science "Earth science"), [statistics](/wiki/Computational_statistics "Computational statistics"), [philosophy](/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), and [logic](/wiki/Logic "Logic").

Computer science is considered by some to have a much closer relationship with mathematics than many scientific disciplines, with some observers saying that computing is a mathematical science.[[34]](#cite_note-Denning-36) Early computer science was strongly influenced by the work of mathematicians such as [Kurt Gödel](/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del "Kurt Gödel"), [Alan Turing](/wiki/Alan_Turing "Alan Turing"), [John von Neumann](/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann"), [Rózsa Péter](/wiki/R%C3%B3zsa_P%C3%A9ter "Rózsa Péter"), [Stephen Kleene](/wiki/Stephen_Cole_Kleene "Stephen Cole Kleene"), and [Alonzo Church](/wiki/Alonzo_Church "Alonzo Church") and there continues to be a useful interchange of ideas between the two fields in areas such as [mathematical logic](/wiki/Mathematical_logic "Mathematical logic"), [category theory](/wiki/Category_theory "Category theory"), [domain theory](/wiki/Domain_theory "Domain theory"), and [algebra](/wiki/Algebra "Algebra").[[37]](#cite_note-Tedre2014-39)

The relationship between computer science and software engineering is a contentious issue, which is further muddied by [disputes](/wiki/Software_engineering#Suitability "Software engineering") over what the term "software engineering" means, and how computer science is defined.[[48]](#cite_note-51) [David Parnas](/wiki/David_Parnas "David Parnas"), taking a cue from the relationship between other engineering and science disciplines, has claimed that the principal focus of computer science is studying the properties of computation in general, while the principal focus of software engineering is the design of specific computations to achieve practical goals, making the two separate but complementary disciplines.[[49]](#cite_note-52)

The academic, political, and funding aspects of computer science tend to depend on whether a department is formed with a mathematical emphasis or with an engineering emphasis. Computer science departments with a mathematics emphasis and with a numerical orientation consider alignment with [computational science](/wiki/Computational_science "Computational science"). Both types of departments tend to make efforts to bridge the field educationally if not across all research.

Philosophy
----------

Main article: [Philosophy of computer science](/wiki/Philosophy_of_computer_science "Philosophy of computer science")

### Epistemology of computer science

Despite the word *science* in its name, there is debate over whether or not computer science is a discipline of science,[[50]](#cite_note-Luk20-53) mathematics,[[51]](#cite_note-Knuth74-54) or engineering.[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55) [Allen Newell](/wiki/Allen_Newell "Allen Newell") and [Herbert A. Simon](/wiki/Herbert_A._Simon "Herbert A. Simon") argued in 1975,

> Computer science is an empirical discipline. We would have called it an experimental science, but like astronomy, economics, and geology, some of its unique forms of observation and experience do not fit a narrow stereotype of the experimental method. Nonetheless, they are experiments. Each new machine that is built is an experiment. Actually constructing the machine poses a question to nature; and we listen for the answer by observing the machine in operation and analyzing it by all analytical and measurement means available.[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55)

It has since been argued that computer science can be classified as an empirical science since it makes use of empirical testing to evaluate the [correctness of programs](/wiki/Correctness_(computer_science) "Correctness (computer science)"), but a problem remains in defining the laws and theorems of computer science (if any exist) and defining the nature of experiments in computer science.[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55) Proponents of classifying computer science as an engineering discipline argue that the reliability of computational systems is investigated in the same way as bridges in [civil engineering](/wiki/Civil_engineering "Civil engineering") and airplanes in [aerospace engineering](/wiki/Aerospace_engineering "Aerospace engineering").[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55) They also argue that while empirical sciences observe what presently exists, computer science observes what is possible to exist and while scientists discover laws from observation, no proper laws have been found in computer science and it is instead concerned with creating phenomena.[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55)

Proponents of classifying computer science as a mathematical discipline argue that computer programs are physical realizations of mathematical entities and programs that can be [deductively reasoned](/wiki/Deductive_reasoning "Deductive reasoning") through mathematical [formal methods](/wiki/Formal_methods "Formal methods").[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55) Computer scientists [Edsger W. Dijkstra](/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra "Edsger W. Dijkstra") and [Tony Hoare](/wiki/Tony_Hoare "Tony Hoare") regard instructions for computer programs as mathematical sentences and interpret formal semantics for programming languages as mathematical [axiomatic systems](/wiki/Axiomatic_system "Axiomatic system").[[52]](#cite_note-plato-55)

### Paradigms of computer science

A number of computer scientists have argued for the distinction of three separate paradigms in computer science. [Peter Wegner](/wiki/Peter_Wegner_(computer_scientist) "Peter Wegner (computer scientist)") argued that those paradigms are science, technology, and mathematics.[[53]](#cite_note-56) [Peter Denning](/wiki/Peter_J._Denning "Peter J. Denning")'s working group argued that they are theory, abstraction (modeling), and design.[[34]](#cite_note-Denning-36) Amnon H. Eden described them as the "rationalist paradigm" (which treats computer science as a branch of mathematics, which is prevalent in theoretical computer science, and mainly employs deductive reasoning), the "technocratic paradigm" (which might be found in engineering approaches, most prominently in software engineering), and the "scientific paradigm" (which approaches computer-related artifacts from the empirical perspective of [natural sciences](/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science"),[[54]](#cite_note-denning07-57) identifiable in some branches of [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence")).[[55]](#cite_note-58) Computer science focuses on methods involved in design, specification, programming, verification, implementation and testing of human-made computing systems.[[56]](#cite_note-59)

As a discipline, computer science spans a range of topics from theoretical studies of algorithms and the limits of computation to the practical issues of implementing computing systems in hardware and software.[[57]](#cite_note-CSAB1997-60)[[58]](#cite_note-61) [CSAB](/wiki/CSAB_(professional_organization) "CSAB (professional organization)"), formerly called Computing Sciences Accreditation Board—which is made up of representatives of the [Association for Computing Machinery](/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery "Association for Computing Machinery") (ACM), and the [IEEE Computer Society](/wiki/IEEE_Computer_Society "IEEE Computer Society") (IEEE CS)[[59]](#cite_note-62)—identifies four areas that it considers crucial to the discipline of computer science: *theory of computation*, *algorithms and data structures*, *programming methodology and languages*, and *computer elements and architecture*. In addition to these four areas, CSAB also identifies fields such as software engineering, artificial intelligence, computer networking and communication, database systems, parallel computation, distributed computation, human–computer interaction, computer graphics, operating systems, and numerical and [symbolic computation](/wiki/Symbolic_computation "Symbolic computation") as being important areas of computer science.[[57]](#cite_note-CSAB1997-60)

### Theoretical computer science

Main article: [Theoretical computer science](/wiki/Theoretical_computer_science "Theoretical computer science")

*Theoretical computer science* is mathematical and abstract in spirit, but it derives its motivation from practical and everyday computation. It aims to understand the nature of computation and, as a consequence of this understanding, provide more efficient methodologies.

#### Theory of computation

Main article: [Theory of computation](/wiki/Theory_of_computation "Theory of computation")

According to Peter Denning, the fundamental question underlying computer science is, "What can be automated?"[[3]](#cite_note-DenningAutomated-3) Theory of computation is focused on answering fundamental questions about what can be computed and what amount of resources are required to perform those computations. In an effort to answer the first question, [computability theory](/wiki/Computability_theory "Computability theory") examines which computational problems are solvable on various theoretical [models of computation](/wiki/Models_of_computation "Models of computation"). The second question is addressed by [computational complexity theory](/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory "Computational complexity theory"), which studies the time and space costs associated with different approaches to solving a multitude of computational problems.

The famous [P = NP?](/wiki/P_%3D_NP%3F "P = NP?") problem, one of the [Millennium Prize Problems](/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems "Millennium Prize Problems"),[[60]](#cite_note-63) is an open problem in the theory of computation.

#### Information and coding theory

#### Programming language theory and formal methods

Main articles: [Programming language theory](/wiki/Programming_language_theory "Programming language theory") and [Formal methods](/wiki/Formal_methods "Formal methods")

Programming language theory is a branch of computer science that deals with the design, implementation, analysis, characterization, and classification of [programming languages](/wiki/Programming_language "Programming language") and their individual [features](/wiki/Programming_language#Elements "Programming language"). It falls within the discipline of computer science, both depending on and affecting mathematics, software engineering, and [linguistics](/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics"). It is an active research area, with numerous dedicated academic journals.

Formal methods are a particular kind of mathematically based technique for the [specification](/wiki/Formal_specification "Formal specification"), development and [verification](/wiki/Formal_verification "Formal verification") of software and [hardware](/wiki/Computer_hardware "Computer hardware") systems.[[63]](#cite_note-66) The use of formal methods for software and hardware design is motivated by the expectation that, as in other engineering disciplines, performing appropriate mathematical analysis can contribute to the reliability and robustness of a design. They form an important theoretical underpinning for software engineering, especially where safety or security is involved. Formal methods are a useful adjunct to software testing since they help avoid errors and can also give a framework for testing. For industrial use, tool support is required. However, the high cost of using formal methods means that they are usually only used in the development of high-integrity and [life-critical systems](/wiki/Life-critical_system "Life-critical system"), where safety or [security](/wiki/Computer_security "Computer security") is of utmost importance. Formal methods are best described as the application of a fairly broad variety of [theoretical computer science](/wiki/Theoretical_computer_science "Theoretical computer science") fundamentals, in particular [logic](/wiki/Logic_in_computer_science "Logic in computer science") calculi, [formal languages](/wiki/Formal_language "Formal language"), [automata theory](/wiki/Automata_theory "Automata theory"), and [program semantics](/wiki/Program_semantics "Program semantics"), but also [type systems](/wiki/Type_systems "Type systems") and [algebraic data types](/wiki/Algebraic_data_types "Algebraic data types") to problems in software and hardware specification and verification.

#### Artificial intelligence

Main articles: [Artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") and [Bio-inspired computing](/wiki/Bio-inspired_computing "Bio-inspired computing")

Artificial intelligence (AI) aims to or is required to synthesize goal-orientated processes such as problem-solving, decision-making, environmental adaptation, learning, and communication found in humans and animals. From its origins in [cybernetics](/wiki/Cybernetics "Cybernetics") and in the [Dartmouth Conference](/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence "History of artificial intelligence") (1956), artificial intelligence research has been necessarily cross-disciplinary, drawing on areas of expertise such as [applied mathematics](/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics"), symbolic logic, [semiotics](/wiki/Semiotics "Semiotics"), [electrical engineering](/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering"), [philosophy of mind](/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind "Philosophy of mind"), [neurophysiology](/wiki/Neurophysiology "Neurophysiology"), and [social intelligence](/wiki/Social_intelligence "Social intelligence"). AI is associated in the popular mind with [robotic development](/wiki/Robotics "Robotics"), but the main field of practical application has been as an embedded component in areas of [software development](/wiki/Software_development "Software development"), which require computational understanding. The starting point in the late 1940s was Alan Turing's question "[Can computers think?](/wiki/Computing_Machinery_and_Intelligence "Computing Machinery and Intelligence")", and the question remains effectively unanswered, although the [Turing test](/wiki/Turing_test "Turing test") is still used to assess computer output on the scale of human intelligence. But the automation of evaluative and predictive tasks has been increasingly successful as a substitute for human monitoring and intervention in domains of computer application involving complex real-world data.

#### Concurrent, parallel and distributed computing

Main articles: [Concurrency (computer science)](/wiki/Concurrency_(computer_science) "Concurrency (computer science)") and [Distributed computing](/wiki/Distributed_computing "Distributed computing")

Concurrency is a property of systems in which several computations are executing simultaneously, and potentially interacting with each other.[[67]](#cite_note-70) A number of mathematical models have been developed for general concurrent computation including [Petri nets](/wiki/Petri_net "Petri net"), [process calculi](/wiki/Process_calculi "Process calculi") and the [parallel random access machine](/wiki/Parallel_random_access_machine "Parallel random access machine") model.[[68]](#cite_note-71) When multiple computers are connected in a network while using concurrency, this is known as a distributed system. Computers within that distributed system have their own private memory, and information can be exchanged to achieve common goals.[[69]](#cite_note-72)

#### Computer networks

Main article: [Computer network](/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network")

This branch of computer science aims studies the construction and behavior of computer networks. It addresses their performance, resilience, security, scalability, and cost-effectiveness, along with the variety of services they can provide.[[70]](#cite_note-73)

#### Computer security and cryptography

Main articles: [Computer security](/wiki/Computer_security "Computer security") and [Cryptography](/wiki/Cryptography "Cryptography")

Computer security is a branch of computer technology with the objective of protecting information from unauthorized access, disruption, or modification while maintaining the accessibility and usability of the system for its intended users.

Historical [cryptography](/wiki/Cryptography "Cryptography") is the art of writing and deciphering secret messages. Modern cryptography is the scientific study of problems relating to distributed computations that can be attacked.[[71]](#cite_note-74) Technologies studied in modern cryptography include symmetric and asymmetric [encryption](/wiki/Encryption "Encryption"), [digital signatures](/wiki/Digital_signature "Digital signature"), [cryptographic hash functions](/wiki/Cryptographic_hash_function "Cryptographic hash function"), [key-agreement protocols](/wiki/Key-agreement_protocol "Key-agreement protocol"), [blockchain](/wiki/Blockchain "Blockchain"), [zero-knowledge proofs](/wiki/Zero-knowledge_proof "Zero-knowledge proof"), and [garbled circuits](/wiki/Garbled_circuit "Garbled circuit").

A database is intended to organize, store, and retrieve large amounts of data easily. Discoveries
-----------

The philosopher of computing [Bill Rapaport](/wiki/William_J._Rapaport "William J. Rapaport") noted three *Great Insights of Computer Science*:[[72]](#cite_note-75)

* [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz")'s, [George Boole](/wiki/George_Boole "George Boole")'s, [Alan Turing](/wiki/Alan_Turing "Alan Turing")'s, [Claude Shannon](/wiki/Claude_Shannon "Claude Shannon")'s, and [Samuel Morse](/wiki/Samuel_Morse "Samuel Morse")'s insight: there are only *two objects* that a computer has to deal with in order to represent "anything".[[note 4]](#cite_note-76)

:   :   All the information about any computable problem can be represented using only 0 and 1 (or any other bistable pair that can flip-flop between two easily distinguishable states, such as "on/off", "magnetized/de-magnetized", "high-voltage/low-voltage", etc.).

See also: [Digital physics](/wiki/Digital_physics "Digital physics")

* [Alan Turing](/wiki/Alan_Turing "Alan Turing")'s insight: there are only *five actions* that a computer has to perform in order to do "anything".

:   :   Every algorithm can be expressed in a language for a computer consisting of only five basic instructions:[[73]](#cite_note-77)

        * move left one location;
        * move right one location;
        * read symbol at current location;
        * print 0 at current location;
        * print 1 at current location.

See also: [Turing machine](/wiki/Turing_machine "Turing machine")

* [Corrado Böhm](/wiki/Corrado_B%C3%B6hm "Corrado Böhm") and Giuseppe Jacopini's insight: there are only *three ways of combining* these actions (into more complex ones) that are needed in order for a computer to do "anything".[[74]](#cite_note-78)

:   :   Only three rules are needed to combine any set of basic instructions into more complex ones:

        * *sequence*: first do this, then do that;
        * *selection*: IF such-and-such is the case, THEN do this, ELSE do that;
        * *repetition*: WHILE such-and-such is the case, DO this.
    :   The three rules of Boehm's and Jacopini's insight can be further simplified with the use of [goto](/wiki/Goto "Goto") (which means it is more elementary than [structured programming](/wiki/Structured_programming "Structured programming")).

See also: [Structured program theorem](/wiki/Structured_program_theorem "Structured program theorem")

Programming paradigms
---------------------

Main article: [Programming paradigm](/wiki/Programming_paradigm "Programming paradigm")

Programming languages can be used to accomplish different tasks in different ways. Common programming paradigms include:

* [Functional programming](/wiki/Functional_programming "Functional programming"), a style of building the structure and elements of computer programs that treats computation as the evaluation of mathematical functions and avoids state and mutable data. It is a declarative programming paradigm, which means programming is done with expressions or declarations instead of statements.[[75]](#cite_note-79)
* [Imperative programming](/wiki/Imperative_programming "Imperative programming"), a programming paradigm that uses statements that change a program's state.[[76]](#cite_note-80) In much the same way that the imperative mood in natural languages expresses commands, an imperative program consists of commands for the computer to perform. Imperative programming focuses on describing how a program operates.
* [Object-oriented programming](/wiki/Object-oriented_programming "Object-oriented programming"), a programming paradigm based on the concept of "objects", which may contain data, in the form of fields, often known as attributes; and code, in the form of procedures, often known as methods. A feature of objects is that an object's procedures can access and often modify the data fields of the object with which they are associated. Thus object-oriented computer programs are made out of objects that interact with one another.[[77]](#cite_note-81)
* [Service-oriented programming](/wiki/Service-oriented_programming "Service-oriented programming"), a programming paradigm that uses "services" as the unit of computer work, to design and implement integrated business applications and [mission critical](/wiki/Mission_critical "Mission critical") software programs.

Many languages offer support for multiple [paradigms](/wiki/Programming_paradigm "Programming paradigm"), making the distinction more a matter of style than of technical capabilities.[[78]](#cite_note-82)

Research
--------

Further information: [List of computer science conferences](/wiki/List_of_computer_science_conferences "List of computer science conferences") and [Category:Computer science journals](/wiki/Category:Computer_science_journals "Category:Computer science journals")

Conferences are important events for computer science research. During these conferences, researchers from the public and private sectors present their recent work and meet. Unlike in most other academic fields, in computer science, the prestige of [conference papers](/wiki/Conference_papers "Conference papers") is greater than that of journal publications.[[79]](#cite_note-83)[[80]](#cite_note-84) One proposed explanation for this is the quick development of this relatively new field requires rapid review and distribution of results, a task better handled by conferences than by journals.[[81]](#cite_note-85)
